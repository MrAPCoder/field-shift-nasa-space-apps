Field Shift documentation

How It Works

Field Shift runs one historical year of daily NASA weather through a simple soil-water model, one day at a time. Everything on the farm and in the score comes from that calculation.

1. The daily soil-water model

  • The soil is one well-mixed store, 0.6 m deep. Its capacity is the plant-available water of a generic sandy, loamy or clay soil (42, 120 or 72 mm). These are textbook values, not a survey of your field.
  • Each day, NASA precipitation and your irrigation are added. Irrigation is spread evenly over the crop season.
  • Drying demand (reference evapotranspiration, ET) comes from NASA temperature, humidity, sunlight, wind and pressure, using the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith method. It is multiplied by a published crop coefficient for the stage of your season.
  • Below the crop stress line (the FAO-56 depletion threshold), the crop can no longer use water at the full rate. Those are the "stress days".
  • Water above capacity leaves the bottom of the 0.6 m store that day. This is modeled drainage, a bookkeeping quantity, not a measurement of real deep drainage.
  • Water in − water used − drainage = change in storage, closing every day and every month. Each year starts on 1 January with the soil half full (an assumption).

2. The farm score

The score is a provisional, educational rating of one simulated season, out of 100. It is not an assessment of a real farm and does not measure soil health, erosion, yield, profit or crop damage. Every scenario uses the same fixed scale and weights.

  • Seven measures are scored. Credit is linear between the zero point and the target. The weights and targets are teaching conventions, not field-validated thresholds.
  • Irrigation efficiency is a water-balance attribution. The same season is rerun with no irrigation. The irrigated run's extra crop-season ET, extra modeled drainage and extra storage at harvest add up exactly to the irrigation applied. Only the ET share is scored. This shows how much crop water use rose when water was added; it does not trace irrigation water into the plant. The drainage share explains the result and is not counted twice.
  • No irrigation means nothing could be lost, so irrigation efficiency gets full credit. The scale stays the same, so a dry, unirrigated crop still loses up to 50 points on crop water supply.
  • Too much irrigation keeps the crop supplied but loses most of the 30 efficiency points, so it cannot reach 100.
  • "Not scored" is shown instead of a number when:
    • the season crosses New Year, or is shorter than 3 months;
    • more than 25% of the season's precipitation fell on freezing days (snow is not modeled);
    • NASA data is missing;
    • or the score would move by 15 or more points between an empty and a full starting soil. A note appears at 5 or more.

3. Biggest problem, options and priorities

  • The biggest problem is the group losing the most points (at least 3), explained with that season's results.
  • Options are full reruns. Field Shift tests irrigation from 0 to 80 mm/week crossed with bare, residue and cover-crop ground cover, on the same weather, location, year, soil, crop and season. Planting dates are never suggested, because the project has no sourced local planting windows. Soil type is never suggested either, since it is not a management choice.
  • Your priority decides which tested option is shown:
    • Balanced takes the largest score gain that does not materially worsen crop water shortage.
    • Avoid crop water shortage only allows options with no extra stress days and no drop in demand met.
    • Save irrigation water shows the largest irrigation cut that does not lower the score. If that cut worsens shortage, it is labelled as a tradeoff, not as an improvement.
    All options appear side by side in one table.
  • Guardrail. A change that adds 7 or more stress days, cuts crop demand met by 5 percentage points or more, or lowers the worst-two-weeks supply by 10 points or more is a material worsening. It is never the balanced suggestion, however many points it gains.
  • Robustness. Every suggested gain must hold whether the soil started the year empty, half full or full, and in a "dry winter" where no freezing-day precipitation reaches the soil. If no option qualifies, the problem is still named, and the card lists what could be investigated.

4. The demo

Try Demo compares Palouse spring wheat on loamy soil, April–August, with no irrigation against an illustrative 40 mm/week. The 40 mm/week is chosen to show the soil filling and draining, not as a recommendation. The five-step summary, the score, the problem and the tested alternative are all calculated live.

Crop rotationsNot in the simulatorSee below. The rotation evidence and the earlier rotation screen are preserved in the research archive.

Why there is no rotation comparison in the simulator. The only approved rotations are Palouse spring pea → winter wheat → spring wheat, plus two alternatives with winter pea and spring barley. A defensible multi-year water comparison would need three things the project doesn't have:

  • sourced water-use parameters for winter wheat, winter pea and spring barley (only spring wheat, dry pea and potato are sourced);
  • a way to represent the winter phases, which grow through the snow and frozen-ground season this model does not simulate;
  • sourced local planting and harvest dates (the crop records say they "do not establish local planting/harvest dates").

Chaining single-year runs would only relabel independent simulations, so we did not ship it. Rotation outcomes, nitrogen benefits, soil-health changes and yield effects are not estimated anywhere in Field Shift.
